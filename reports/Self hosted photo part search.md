# Embeddings find the drawer, Qwen reads the label

Stack-n-Track can get genuinely useful photo search at $0, but only as a **hybrid**: a frozen image-embedding model (DINOv3-B/L for photo-to-photo, optionally SigLIP2 for text) ranks the whole catalog by visual similarity in milliseconds, and a small Qwen vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-4B/8B-Instruct or Qwen3.5-4B/9B with thinking off) reads printed markings and extracts attributes that feed the existing FTS5 index, with the two rankings merged by reciprocal-rank fusion. Neither half is enough alone. Embeddings reliably find visually distinctive items (dev boards, modules, connectors, ICs on breakouts) and the right *family* of commodity parts, but they cannot tell a 1k resistor from a 10k one or an M3×8 from an M3×12. VLMs read large printed text well but fail at colour bands. Frontier models misread a resistor in every case in one January 2026 test, and a hobbyist test of Gemma 3 27B confused a BMP280 with a BME280. The realistic product is therefore "top-5 suggestions plus one clarifying question for value-only parts." That fits put-back mode well, because the action is undoable and the "Recently taken out" list already narrows the candidates. Everything runs on CPU in the existing Node container for the embedding half (transformers.js with ONNX DINOv3/SigLIP2 builds, brute-force cosine in SQLite). The VLM half reuses the user's existing Qwen serving stack, ideally Ollama, at roughly 2–5 s per photo on an 8 GB card. No published benchmark covers electronic-part retrieval, so the decision rests on a cheap offline evaluation on the user's own parts: about 50 items photographed in two sessions. The go bar is R@5 ≥ 0.8 on modules/ICs/connectors, R@5 ≥ 0.7 overall, and photo search must beat the recency list alone.

## Global embeddings find the part family, not the value printed on it

The capability question splits cleanly by category, and the split is driven by physics more than by model choice. Modern global embeddings encode shape, colour and texture. A dev board with silkscreen, a distinctive layout and headers is a visually unique object. Matching a query photo against the user's own catalog photo of that same board is classic instance retrieval, which the current generation of models handles well. A 1/4 W through-hole resistor, by contrast, differs from its siblings only in a few millimetres of coloured bands whose hue shifts with lighting and phone white balance. At the 224–512 px the models actually see, that difference is nearly invisible. The prior art confirms the pattern. Academic component classifiers report **94–99% accuracy** on clean datasets, but only for coarse classes ("resistor vs capacitor") ([DeepTronic](https://animorepository.dlsu.edu.ph/faculty_research/3025); [FICS-PCB survey](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2506.19330)). The hard problem here is the sibling-within-a-category problem, and no published benchmark measures it.

Reading values is no easier for VLMs. In a January 2026 EEVblog thread, ChatGPT, Grok and Google AI all gave wrong values for the same resistor (**11 MΩ, 10 MΩ and 82 Ω**). They did so with high confidence, and Copilot only got it right after a human typed in the band colours ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/resistor-help-colorblind-need-help-reading-resistor-color/)). Classical OpenCV readers manage only **25–75% on 5-band resistors**, even with a tripod and softbox ([Telkom University thesis](https://openlibrary.telkomuniversity.ac.id/pustaka/files/154041/abstract/aplikasi-android-pembaca-nilai-resistor-menggunakan-opencv.pdf)). Printed text is more tractable but still error-prone at the character level: a Gemma 3 27B test read codes like "103" and "221" but **misidentified a BMP280 as a BME280** ([elektroda.com](https://www.elektroda.com/news/news4113352.html)). Gauge reading is a close analogue of fine-grained visual measurement, and there the best VLM scores only about **30% on real-world images** ([MeasureBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26865v2)). Screws cannot be sized from an uncalibrated photo at all. Every screw-ID app relies on a coin or ruler in frame, and vendors still recommend calipers ([Screw Identifier](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6759244718); [Magica](https://magica.com/features/ai-screw-identifier)).

The table below shows how the evidence maps onto the parts in the catalog. It is a design assumption to be confirmed by the evaluation, not a measured result.

| Part type | What vision can realistically deliver | Where the final answer comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Dev boards, modules, breakouts | Specific item, often top-1 (unique layout, silkscreen) | Embedding match |
| Connectors, distinctive hardware | Specific item or a small family | Embedding match, user picks from top 5 |
| ICs (DIP/SOIC) | Package type from embedding; part number only from a sharp, glare-free close-up | VLM OCR → exact-text match against attributes |
| Electrolytic caps | Family from embedding; value often readable on the sleeve | VLM OCR of the value |
| Resistors, MLCCs, small ceramics | "A resistor / ceramic cap", not the value | One clarifying question (meter reading or typed code) |
| Screws, nuts, washers | Head type and drive; not thread or length | Clarifying question, or a mm-grid mat in the photo |

Embedding search should therefore be built and judged as a **candidate generator** that puts the right item in the top five, with text (OCR, attributes, or one typed answer) deciding among near-identical siblings. That is the same structure eBay used in production: predict category, recall by visual similarity, re-rank by aspects ([Visual Search at eBay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03154)). The use case appears to be unbuilt in self-hosted inventory tools. Homebox Companion and Homebox AI Frontend use VLMs to *catalog* new items from photos, not to retrieve existing ones ([AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/software/homebox-companion/about); [Homebox AI Frontend](https://git.hswro.org/mab122/homebox_ai_fronted/raw/branch/master/README.md)), and no AI photo features turned up for InvenTree or Part-DB ([InvenTree docs](https://git.invenhost.com/inventree/InvenTree/src/commit/26b5f0ae4c37e558054323d5265dc1117a0bf4b2/docs/docs/part/index.md)).

## DINOv3 leads frozen same-object retrieval; SigLIP2 adds text search

The best evidence for choosing the embedding model is ILIAS (CVPR 2025). It measures instance-level retrieval, meaning query photos of 1,000 specific objects matched against positives taken under different conditions, which is the user's exact task at a vastly larger scale. Among frozen models with no training, **DINOv3-L scores 26.5 mAP@1k at 100M distractors**, ahead of PE-Core-L@336 (22.0), SigLIP2-L@512 (20.8) and DINOv2-L (15.3). Old OpenAI CLIP/OpenCLIP-class models trail at roughly 9–11 ([ILIAS leaderboard](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/); [arXiv 2502.11748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11748)). The small model punches well above its weight. **DINOv3-B (86M params) scores 22.0**, beating SigLIP2-L@512 frozen at a quarter of the size. The old folk wisdom "use DINO, not CLIP, for same-object matching" therefore still holds, but only for DINOv3. DINOv2 now loses to SigLIP2-L.

| Model (frozen) | Params (vision) | ILIAS I2I mAP@1k (100M) | Text queries? | Node ONNX build |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DINOv3-L/16 | 300M | **26.5** | No | onnx-community |
| DINOv3-B/16 | 86M | 22.0 | No | onnx-community |
| PE-Core-L/14@336 | ~320M | 22.0 (33.4 adapted) | Yes | None; Python/timm |
| SigLIP2-L@512 | 303M | 20.8 (31.3 adapted) | Yes, best T2I (19.8) | onnx-community |
| SigLIP2-B@512 | 86M | 15.4 | Yes | onnx-community |
| DINOv2-L | 300M | 15.3 | No | Xenova / onnx-community |
| OpenCLIP ViT-L | 300M | 9.4 | Yes | Xenova / onnx-community |

Sources: [ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/), [DINOv3 card](https://huggingface.co/facebook/dinov3-vitl16-pretrain-lvd1689m), [SigLIP 2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14786), [PE-Core card](https://huggingface.co/timm/PE-Core-L-14-336), [HF onnx-community](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community).

Two caveats temper the table. First, ILIAS objects (products, artworks, landmarks) are far more distinctive than a drawer of resistors, so the family ranking should transfer but the absolute numbers will not. Second, **a cheap learned linear adapter flips the leaderboard**: PE-L climbs to 33.4 and SigLIP2-L to 31.3, while DINOv3-L barely moves (26.5 to 28.3) ([ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/)). That matters later. Once the app has accumulated a few hundred confirmed (query photo, item) pairs, a SigLIP2 adapter trained on them could overtake frozen DINOv3. On day one, with no training data, DINOv3 is the right default. DINOv3 ships under Meta's own license. It permits commercial use and redistribution with a "Built with DINOv3" notice, excludes military and weapons uses, and the official HF repos are gated behind license acceptance ([DINOv3 License](https://ai.meta.com/resources/models-and-libraries/dinov3-license/)). None of that is a problem for a personal app. If an OSI license matters, SigLIP2 and PE-Core are Apache-2.0.

DINOv3 has no text tower, so it cannot answer a typed query like "blue buck converter." SigLIP2-L@512 is the strongest text-to-image model on ILIAS (19.8), nearly matching its own image-to-image score ([ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/)). Text-to-image matters less here than it seems, though. A typed query like "100uF" will match the item's *text fields* through FTS5 far better than any photo, because the value is printed text that vision encoders handle poorly. The clean design is to store a DINOv3 vector per photo for photo search. A SigLIP2 vector is optional, and only worth adding if typed fuzzy queries ("that red thing with the knob") turn out to matter. A single-model compromise is SigLIP2-L@384/512, which does both reasonably. One newcomer is worth watching: **SigLino-30M** (TII, CVPR 2026, Apache-2.0) is distilled from both DINOv3 and SigLIP2 and outputs both heads from one 30M-parameter model. It currently requires Python `trust_remote_code` and has no ONNX build ([HF tiiuae/siglino-30M](https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/siglino-30M)).

Preprocessing matters more than which of the top three models is chosen. ILIAS shows resolution sensitivity (SigLIP-L rises from 15.2 at 256 px to 19.6 at 384 px), and the ILIAS authors identify **background clutter as the major failure mode** ([ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/)). Downscaling a whole 1600 px frame of a workbench to 224 px leaves a tiny SOT-23 as a few pixels. Crop to the object *before* resizing to model input, and index several views per item (full frame, tight crop, 90° rotations and flips, since small parts have no canonical orientation). Score each item by its maximum similarity over its views. Background removal is a genuine trade-off, not a free win. PRISM found segmentation cut false matches from background regions ([PRISM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14985)), but V²L found removing backgrounds *lowered* accuracy by stripping discriminative detail ([V²L](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.12994)). Treat raw, object-crop and background-removed as A/B variants in the evaluation.

## Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 read markings in 2–5 seconds on an 8 GB card

The VLM's job is narrower than "identify the part." It should (a) transcribe any visible text, (b) classify category and package into a fixed schema, (c) optionally return a bounding box to crop on, and (d) optionally rerank a top-5 shortlist. For that job the Qwen family is both the strongest small option and the lowest-friction one, given the user already self-hosts Qwen. **Qwen3.5-9B scores 89.2 on OCRBench and Qwen3.5-4B scores 85.0**, with RefCOCO grounding of 89.7/88.1, all under Apache-2.0 ([HF Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B)). Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct is reported at about 89.6 OCRBench ([Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen3-vl-4b-vs-qwen3-vl-8b-benchmarks-vram-guide/)), and its card highlights OCR "robust to low light, blur, and tilt" ([HF Qwen3-VL-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct)). Qwen3-VL emits grounding boxes in a 0–1000 normalized space ([Qwen3-VL report](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631)), so the same model can say "the part is here" and the app can crop before embedding and OCR. One detail matters for the user's existing deployment: **Qwen3.5 small models are natively multimodal** ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/qwen3-5-small-models)). If the Qwen already running for the other project is a Qwen3.5 4B/9B, the user may already have a capable VLM loaded and need no second model at all.

The only real latency data comes from PhotoPrism's August 2026 benchmark on an **RTX 4060 8 GB** (Ollama, 720 px images, 4K context). It measured median label latency of **2.4 s for qwen3-vl:4b-instruct, 2.5 s for qwen3.5:4b, 3.3 s for qwen3.5:9b and 5.2 s for qwen3-vl:8b-instruct**. Asking for more output roughly doubles those (11.3 s for the 8B). Reasoning builds are much slower: a captioning call took 6.4 s vs 1.2 s for the instruct build ([PhotoPrism](https://docs.photoprism.app/developer-guide/vision/model-comparison/)). Qwen3.5 runs in thinking mode by default and needs `enable_thinking=False` ([HF Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B)), which also keeps reasoning text from polluting JSON output. CPU-only VLM inference is a different class of experience: an older PhotoPrism run measured **24–36 s per image** for a 7B model on a Ryzen AI 9 365 (since marked as superseded) ([PhotoPrism](https://docs.photoprism.app/developer-guide/vision/model-comparison/)). On CPU, the VLM is therefore a background enrichment job, not part of the interactive loop.

| Hardware tier | Embeddings | VLM (interactive) | What the user gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPU only | DINOv3-B ONNX in the Node process | None interactive; 1–2B model (qwen3.5:2b, minicpm-v4.6:1b) as an overnight catalog-enrichment batch | Photo top-5 + typed-marking clarify; no live OCR |
| 8 GB | Same, on CPU (keep VRAM for the VLM) | qwen3-vl:4b-instruct (3.3 GB) or qwen3.5:4b (3.4 GB), Q4; swaps with a resident text Qwen | Photo top-5 in ~1–2 s, OCR refinement ~2–5 s later |
| 12–16 GB | DINOv3-L on CPU, or a small GPU sidecar | qwen3-vl:8b-instruct (6.2 GB) or qwen3.5:9b (6.6 GB) Q4, coexisting with the text model; Q8 at 16 GB | Better OCR; VLM rerank of top 5 affordable |
| 24 GB | Optional SigLIP2-L/PE-L GPU sidecar | 8–9B at Q8, or Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B Q4 (20 GB) if nothing else needs the card | Marginal over 12–16 GB for this task |

Sizes are from [Ollama qwen3-vl tags](https://ollama.com/library/qwen3-vl/tags) and [PhotoPrism](https://docs.photoprism.app/developer-guide/vision/model-comparison/). VRAM headroom guidance (4B needs at least 6 GB, 8B is comfortable at 12–16 GB) comes from a secondary guide ([Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen3-vl-4b-vs-qwen3-vl-8b-benchmarks-vram-guide/)). No measured latency exists for a 3060 12 GB, 4060 Ti 16 GB or 3090, so the tiers above the 8 GB row are extrapolations. The 24 GB tier buys little because the bottleneck is image quality and the physics of tiny markings, not model size. That VRAM is better spent keeping an 8B model resident with a generous image-token budget.

Two failure modes need defensive design rather than better models. The first is *valid but wrong* output. PhotoPrism saw zero malformed JSON from qwen2.5vl:7b, but another model returned schema-valid JSON with empty labels on every image ([PhotoPrism](https://docs.photoprism.app/developer-guide/vision/model-comparison/)). Use Ollama's `format` JSON-schema parameter, which works with vision models ([Ollama structured outputs](https://docs.ollama.com/capabilities/structured-outputs)), and include `unknown`/null options, a legibility self-rating, and the raw transcribed text stored separately from any parsed value. The second is decoding. Colour bands and EIA-96 SMD codes are lookup problems, so the VLM should transcribe colours or codes and deterministic TypeScript should decode them. The decoded value should only ever be shown as "suggested, please verify."

## One SQLite file, one Node process, one shared Ollama

The serving picture is unusually favourable for a Node stack. **Transformers.js 4.x runs DINOv2, DINOv3, SigLIP2 and CLIP ONNX models in-process on onnxruntime-node**, on CPU or CUDA 12 on Linux x64 ([transformers.js ONNX backend](https://app.unpkg.com/@huggingface/transformers/files/src/backends/onnx.js)). The onnx-community org hosts `dinov3-vitb16-pretrain-lvd1689m-ONNX`, the vitl16 variant, and official SigLIP2 builds from base to so400m ([HF onnx-community](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community)). One researcher's note claimed SigLIP2 had only community conversions, but the HF API listing shows onnx-community builds, and the more direct evidence wins. The embedding call is `pipeline("image-feature-extraction", repo, { dtype: "q8" })` ([onnx-community example](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community/ijepa_vith14_22k)). The dedicated embedding servers mostly don't fit. Ollama still has **no image-embedding support**: issue #5304 has been open since June 2024 ([ollama#5304](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/issues/5304)). HF TEI is text-only ([TEI supported models](https://huggingface.co/docs/text-embeddings-inference/supported_models)), and Infinity v3 requires an Ampere-or-newer GPU ([Infinity](https://github.com/michaelfeil/infinity)). Keep embeddings in the app container on CPU, and keep the app image slim and CUDA-free.

Vector search needs no infrastructure at this scale. sqlite-vec brute-forces **100k vectors of ≤1024 dims in under 75 ms** on an M1 Mac mini and loads into better-sqlite3 with one call ([sqlite-vec blog](https://alexgarcia.xyz/blog/2024/sqlite-vec-stable-release/index.html); [sqlite-vec JS](https://alexgarcia.xyz/sqlite-vec/js.html)), so a few thousand photo vectors take a few milliseconds. A plain Float32Array cosine loop in TypeScript is equally valid and avoids a native extension. If sqlite-vec is used, build on a glibc base image (`node:22-slim`), not Alpine, which is a likely but unverified prebuilt-binary concern. The schema change is a `photo_embeddings` table keyed by photo, with `item_id`, `model`, `view` (full/crop/rot90…) and a BLOB vector. Allowing **many reference photos per item** is what makes the feedback loop work.

The VLM goes over HTTP to whatever serves the existing Qwen. If that is Ollama, add the vision model to the same instance. Ollama loads models concurrently when they fit and otherwise queues and evicts idle ones. A short per-request `keep_alive` (e.g. `"30s"`) stops the VLM squatting on the other project's VRAM ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq)). If the existing Qwen runs in llama.cpp, llama-server supports Qwen2.5/3-VL through `--mmproj` with an OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint, but serves one model per process, so add a second server or a swap proxy ([llama.cpp multimodal](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/docs/multimodal.md)). vLLM is a poor neighbour on a shared consumer card because it preallocates GPU memory up front ([vLLM memory docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/configuration/conserving_memory.html)). If GPU embeddings are ever wanted, put them in a separate Compose service with a `deploy.resources.reservations.devices` GPU reservation ([Docker Compose GPU](https://docs.docker.com/compose/how-tos/gpu-support/)).

The query path in put-back mode should be progressive, because users stay engaged for about 1 s and lose attention around 10 s ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). The phone already sends a 1600 px JPEG. The server embeds it and runs kNN to produce **ranking A**, then fuses it immediately with a recency prior from the "Recently taken out" list, and returns top-5 cards. Meanwhile the VLM runs asynchronously and returns category, package and `visible_text`. An FTS5 `bm25()` query over that text produces **ranking B**. A and B are merged with RRF, `score = Σ 1/(60 + rank)`. RRF needs no score normalization between cosine and BM25, and k=60 is the published default that was "not altered" during validation ([Cormack et al. 2009](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)). Three rules sit on top of the fusion. An **exact-text pin** puts an item at rank 1 when OCR matches a part-number attribute exactly ("NE555", "AMS1117-3.3"). A confident VLM category acts as a **soft boost, never a hard filter**, because a wrong hard filter zeroes recall. A **single clarifying question** fires when several same-category value-only parts crowd the top 5, or when the top-1/top-2 margin is small: "What's printed on it?", "What does your meter read?", or "Length in mm?" The UI shows what was read ("Read: 'LM358'") so the user can correct it, and the cards reorder in place. When the user confirms a put-back, the query photo is appended as another reference photo for that item. That **enrollment** steadily fills the catalog with photos taken under real garage conditions, which directly attacks the background-bias problem that degrades metric-learning retrieval ([CORE: background bias](https://core.ac.uk/works/131750347)). Because put-back is undoable, a wrong top-1 costs a tap. That makes R@5, not R@1, the metric that decides whether the feature is worth having.

The recency prior is this report's own inference rather than something the research notes measured, but it is probably the single biggest accuracy lever available. Most put-backs are of something recently taken out. Fusing the "Recently taken out" list as a third RRF ranking, or as a boost, shrinks the effective search space from thousands of items to a handful whenever it applies, and it costs nothing.

## A 50-item, two-session evaluation decides go or no-go

No published benchmark covers small-catalog, phone-photo, electronic-part retrieval, so the decision has to come from the user's own parts. The protocol is cheap. Pick **30–50 items stratified by category**, roughly eight each of resistors, capacitors, ICs, modules/boards, connectors and screws/nuts. Deliberately include **near-duplicate groups**, such as three resistor values, NE555 vs LM358 in DIP-8, and M3×8 vs M3×12, because those cases decide whether the feature feels trustworthy. Use the existing catalog photos as the gallery where they exist (that is the honest test). Then shoot query photos on a different day with a different background, light and angle, under real put-back conditions, because changing backgrounds between query and gallery sharply degrades retrieval ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/131750347)). Add about 10 parts that are *not* in the catalog to measure false confidence. Always search the **full catalog**, not just the 50, so the distractors are realistic.

Cache every embedding and VLM output once. All fusion, filter, crop and recency variants then re-run offline in milliseconds, so the ablation grid costs almost nothing. That grid covers DINOv3-B vs DINOv3-L vs SigLIP2-L, raw vs object-crop vs background-removed, embedding-only vs VLM-text-only vs RRF fusion, with and without the category boost and recency prior, and, last and only if needed, VLM rerank of the top 5. Report **R@1, R@5 and MRR** overall and per category, plus median and p95 latency per stage, clarify rate, and the false-confident rate on out-of-catalog parts. Industrial teams pair offline metrics with human relevance judgments before trusting them ([Shop The Look](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.10866)). Here the user is the judge.

The sample is small, so read results with statistical humility. At n = 50 each query is 2 percentage points, and the 95% interval on an R@5 near 0.8 is roughly ±11 pp. Differences under about 10 pp are noise unless a paired count (queries variant X wins and Y loses, McNemar-style) is lopsided. The thresholds below are recommendations from the pipeline research, not an industry standard. The recency baseline in particular is this report's addition.

| Gate | Threshold | Consequence if missed |
|---|---|---|
| Distinctive parts (modules, ICs, connectors) | R@5 ≥ 0.8, R@1 ≥ 0.5 | No-go for photo search as a headline feature |
| Overall, vision + text fusion | R@5 ≥ 0.7 | No-go; revisit cropping and capture guidance |
| Value-only parts (resistors, caps, screws) | R@5 ≥ 0.9 *after one clarifying answer* | Ship those categories as "category + question" only |
| Beats "Recently taken out" list alone | Fused R@5 clearly above recency-only R@5 | Photo search adds nothing in put-back; stop |
| Out-of-catalog parts | Low false-confident rate at the chosen margin threshold | Add an explicit "none of these / new item" path |
| VLM rerank | +10 pp R@1 within latency budget | Leave rerank off |

One more measurement makes the self-improving claim testable. After the first round, add the query-session photos as enrolled references, shoot a third session, and re-run. If enrollment lifts R@5 materially, the feature gets better with use, and early mediocre numbers are less damning.

## Four phases from an evening's script to a self-improving index

**Phase 0 is an offline harness, and it is the go/no-go.** Write a standalone Node script using `@huggingface/transformers` that embeds every existing catalog photo with DINOv3-B and DINOv3-L (and SigLIP2-L@384 for comparison) on CPU. Take about 50 query photos, compute R@1/R@5/MRR per category and against the recency baseline, and time per-image CPU embedding, since no citable CPU latency figure exists for these models on consumer hardware. Also try the three crop variants. No app changes and no GPU are needed. If DINOv3 cannot put distinctive parts in the top 5 at least 80% of the time here, no amount of VLM cleverness will rescue the feature, and the user has spent an evening, not a month.

**Phase 1 ships embedding-only photo search in put-back mode.** It adds the `photo_embeddings` table, a background job to embed existing and new photos with crops and rotations, an upload endpoint returning the top 5 fused with the recency list via RRF, and enrollment on confirmed put-back. This runs entirely in the existing container on CPU, works in every hardware tier, and is useful on its own for boards, modules and connectors.

**Phase 2 adds the Qwen reader.** Call the existing Ollama (or a second llama-server) with qwen3-vl:4b-instruct on 8 GB, or the 8B/qwen3.5:9b with thinking off on 12 GB and up, using a JSON schema for category, package, visible_text, legibility and an optional bounding box. Feed `visible_text` into FTS5, fuse, apply the exact-text pin and the soft category boost, and add the one-question clarify flow with deterministic colour-band and SMD-code decoders. Run the same extraction once over the whole catalog as an overnight batch so that catalog items gain searchable OCR text too. On a CPU-only box, this batch enrichment is the *only* VLM use. Re-run the Phase 0 evaluation with the VLM text ranking included, and keep Phase 2 only if fusion beats embedding-only on ICs and caps.

**Phase 3 is optional and evidence-gated.** One option is VLM rerank of the top 5 when the margin is small, kept only if it adds 10 pp of R@1. Another is local-feature re-ranking, which gave the largest gains on ILIAS (an adapted SigLIP-L rose from 28.9 to 38.9 with the AMES re-ranker; [ILIAS](https://vrg.fel.cvut.cz/ilias/)), though LightGlue-style matcher licensing was not verified. A third option arrives once a few hundred confirmed pairs exist: a linear adapter on SigLIP2-L or PE-Core-L, the configuration that tops ILIAS when adapted. A printed mm-grid capture mat for screws is a cheap physical fix that no model replaces.

## Conclusion

The research shifts what the right question is. "Can AI identify my unlabeled part?" has a clear no for the commodity parts that make up most of a drawer. Neither embeddings nor frontier VLMs can read resistor bands or size screws reliably. "Can AI get the right item into five tappable cards, and ask one good question when it can't?" is a much better question, and the evidence suggests yes, cheaply, on hardware the user already has. Three facts make that version of the feature stronger than the bare model scores imply. First, the search space in put-back mode is mostly the recently-taken-out list, not the whole catalog. Second, every confirmed put-back adds a real-conditions reference photo, so accuracy improves with use. Third, the undo button makes a wrong guess nearly free.

The highest-value next step needs no GPU, no VLM and no schema change: a Phase 0 script that embeds the existing catalog with DINOv3 and scores 50 fresh photos against it and against the recency list. That result settles whether the feature is worth building. It also settles the hardware question. If embeddings plus recency already clear the bar, the GPU tier matters only for the OCR refinement on ICs and capacitors. If the existing Qwen deployment turns out to be a natively multimodal Qwen3.5, even that may cost nothing extra.
